Aristotle establishes at the beginning of the *Nicomachean Ethics* that the political end is superior to the individual end: "[politics] ordains which of the sciences should be studied in a state, and which each class of citizens should learn and up to what point they should learn them"[^1]. Having already established that the "ends of the master arts are to be preferred to all the subordinate ends; for it is for the sake of the former that the latter is pursued"[^2], the Stagirite states that, as politics makes use of the rest of the sciences, and "legislates as to what we are to do and what we are to abstain from"[^3], the ends of the different sciences must be included in the end of politics, therefore, politics is the master art and its end, the chief good. 

Moreover, Aristotle gives another reason for considering the political good as superior to the individual good. Even if we consider the ends of both individual men and states to be the same, he says, "that of the state seems at all events something greater and more complete", due to the fact that attaining the end of the state  is "finer and more godlike"[^4]. 

Furthermore, Aristotle argues that "the state is a creation of nature" and "man is a political animal"[^8], and the *eudaimonia* of man requires the social and political sphere. He explicitly states that self-sufficiency is not to be taken in an atomized meaning: "Now, by self-sufficient we do not mean that which is sufficient for a man by himself, for one who lives a solitary life, but also for parents, children, wife, and, in general for his friends and fellow citizens, since man is born for citizenship"[^9].

On the contrary, Aristotle himself says that the end that we desire for its own sake and not for the sake of something else must be the ultimate good: "not all ends are final ends; but the chief good is evidently something final... we call final without qualification that which is ***always desirable in itself and never for the sake of something else***"[^5], which he then identifies with *eudaimonia*: "Happiness, then, is something final and self-sufficient, and is the end of action" and he proceeds to equate it with the "activity of the soul in accordance with virtue"[^7]. In addition to that, in *Politics*, Aristotle concludes that "the state comes into existence, originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence ***for the sake of a good life***"[^6].

Given the fact that a state cannot engage in the "activity of the soul in accordance with virtue", and that the end (and for the sake of argument, its origin) of politics is the *eudaimonia* of its members, its end (*telos*, aim) is the individual good. To phrase it differently, the political good is subordinate to the individual good. Even stating that the state continues in existence "for the sake of a good life" directly rules out the possibility of the political good as something ultimate, being that it is *at the service of* the individual good, the ultimate good, the *eudaimonia* of its members; the individual good remains superior to the political in a *teleological* manner.

Having established this distinction, we can see how the initial arguments for the supremacy of the political good can be resolved: politics is a master science over many subordinate arts and sciences, but it is not pursued *for its own sake*. While Aristotle's insight about the individual not being self-sufficient, and the political and familial spheres being an essential part of  the *eudaimonia* of men, is true, the whole purpose or *telos* of the state consists in the flourishing of its members, both as providing the necessary conditions for it and by being an integral part of the flourishing itself. Finally, Aristotle refers to achieving the end of the state as something "finer and more godlike", in the sense that it encompasses the good of a whole community instead of a single individual, not implying that the political is superior to the individual regarding its *telos*.
